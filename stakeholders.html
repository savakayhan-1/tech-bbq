Who matters, and how to involve them.

A multi-stakeholder engagement map for TechBBQ's international partnerships, building on my MBA thesis on multi-stakeholder engagement.

A two-step model.

First, a power–interest grid shows where each stakeholder sits. Then an engagement ladder decides how deeply to involve them. Every mechanism below is a format TechBBQ already runs.

The power–interest grid (Mendelow)

Developed by Aubrey Mendelow, it sorts stakeholders by two questions: how much power they have over an outcome, and how much they care about it. The answers place each one in one of four boxes: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, or monitor.

The IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation

Developed by the International Association for Public Participation (IAP2), it's one of the most widely used frameworks in stakeholder engagement. It describes five levels of involvement, each giving stakeholders more say than the last: inform, consult, involve, collaborate and empower.

Why I chose these two

  • Together they answer both questions a partnerships team faces. The grid says who deserves the most time; the spectrum says how to work with each of them. One without the other is either a list or a method with no focus.
  • Partnership isn't a yes-or-no. Most frameworks treat someone as a partner or not. The IAP2 spectrum shows the steps in between, which is how TechBBQ partners actually grow: the Danish Life Science Cluster moved from programme partner to advisory board member to funding its own DKK 750,000 area.
  • They scale to hundreds of stakeholders. With 222 partners and many more organisations around the summit, the team needs a simple way to decide where to spend its time. Both tools are quick to apply and easy to share with colleagues.
  • They turn analysis into action. Each rung of the ladder maps onto a real TechBBQ format, from newsletters to advisory boards to self-funded areas, so the model ends in a concrete proposal, not a diagram.
  • They build on my MBA thesis. My research focused on multi-stakeholder engagement, and these frameworks let me apply it to TechBBQ in a way any colleague can follow.

The full landscape.

Partners, attendees and volunteers are the visible part. Around TechBBQ sit seven groups of stakeholders, each with its own reason to be there.

Funders

222 paying partners across seven tiers; foundations such as Novo Nordisk Foundation, Industriens Fond and Bestseller Foundation; public funders such as Erhvervsfremmebestyrelsen and EIFO; ticket buyers.

Content makers

Startups exhibiting and pitching; 300+ speakers and moderators; advisory boards; programme partners hosting their own sessions and side events, such as Google Cloud, NVIDIA and Boardway.

Capital

VCs, corporate investors and angels (e.g. DanBAN); LPs, family offices, pension funds and insurers (e.g. PFA, ATP, Keva); public investors such as EIF and EIFO.

Government and diplomacy

Danish ministries (two ministers spoke in 2026); the City of Copenhagen; EU institutions; foreign governments and agencies such as India's DPIIT; embassies; royal patronage at the Royal Reception.

Ecosystem

Universities and research (DTU, University of Copenhagen, Innovation District Copenhagen, BioInnovation Institute); clusters and associations (Danish Life Science Cluster, DI); incubators and accelerators; 103 community partners, from women's networks to diaspora groups.

Reach and talent

150+ accredited media, media partners and data providers such as Dealroom; job seekers through Tech Talent; volunteers.

Enablers

Venues (Bella Center, Hotel d'Angleterre, Maersk Tower); platforms (Brella for matchmaking, Tito for tickets, GetAccept for contracts, Airtable); catering, wardrobe and party venues; and TechBBQ's own teams.

Moving partners up the ladder.

A country agency might start with one session (involve), join the corridor's advisory board the next year (collaborate), and fund its own area in year three (empower).

That's close to the path the Danish Life Science Cluster took at TechBBQ: first a programme partner and advisory board member, then in 2025 the funder of its own DKK 750,000 exhibition area. Every proposal I write would aim to create value for at least two corners of the map at once.

Measuring the return for each stakeholder.

Every stakeholder judges a partnership by its own idea of return. A corporate looks at revenue, a foundation at impact, a country agency at what its startups achieved. So the measurement has to start from each partner's objectives.

ROI: return on investment

Measures the return generated relative to the investment made. Used where the return can be given a value: revenue, deals, the worth of qualified leads.

ROI = (Return − Investment) ÷ Investment × 100

Example (hypothetical): a partner pays €37,000, and the deals and leads it gets are worth €80,000. ROI = (80,000 − 37,000) ÷ 37,000 × 100 ≈ 116%.

ROO: return on objectives

Not every return has a price tag: brand awareness, a new relationship, a deal that closes next year. For these, I measure results against the objectives agreed at signing: did we hit the targets we set together?

How I'd measure it

  1. Before signing: define the partner's objectives and agree on the KPIs, such as qualified leads, meetings, brand reach, business opportunities or revenue.
  2. During the summit: track the KPIs as they happen, through Brella meeting bookings, booth scans, session sign-ups and the HubSpot record.
  3. After the summit: send a one-page report within four weeks, comparing results with the KPIs and the investment. That report opens the renewal conversation.
